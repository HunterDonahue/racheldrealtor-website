No word on an inspection report causes more panic than "foundation." In a lot of Fort Worth's older neighborhoods, some degree of foundation movement over 60-100 years is close to universal — expansive clay soil does what it does. That doesn't mean every mention of it is a dealbreaker, but it doesn't mean you should ignore it either.

## Why this soil moves in the first place

North Texas clay soil expands when it's wet and contracts when it's dry, and that cycle puts real pressure on a foundation over decades. It's a regional reality, not a sign of a poorly built or poorly maintained home. Almost every older home in this area has had some interaction with it.

## Signs that are usually just cosmetic

Small hairline cracks in drywall, a door that sticks seasonally, slightly uneven flooring in an older section of the house — these are common and often unrelated to any active structural concern. They're worth noting, not panicking over.

## Signs worth a professional opinion

Cracks wider than a quarter inch, doors and windows that have become difficult to operate over a short period of time, or visible separation where a wall meets the ceiling are worth having a licensed foundation engineer evaluate — not a foundation repair company's sales rep, who has an incentive to find work. An independent engineer's report costs a little more upfront and is worth every penny in what it tells you honestly.

## What repairs actually mean for you

A home with a documented, professionally repaired foundation and a transferable warranty is often a safer bet than one that's never been evaluated at all — you actually know what you're getting. The absence of any foundation history isn't necessarily good news; it might just mean nobody's looked closely.
